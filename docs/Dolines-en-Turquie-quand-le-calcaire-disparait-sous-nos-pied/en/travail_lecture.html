Sinkholes in Turkey: when limestone disappears beneath our feet

Instruction. Choose the reading level that suits you. Read the text, then write down the main points in the right-hand column.

Level 1 · Simplified reading

Imagine a maize field in Turkey. The ground opened up one morning. There was neither an earthquake nor an explosion. There is a round hole left. The sides are vertical. The hole is large. People call it an obruk. Scientists call it a sinkhole.

It is a closed depression. It is often round. It forms in rock. Water dissolves it. The rock is often limestone. Limestone produces shells. Limestone produces chalk. Water and carbon dioxide form an acid. This acid attacks limestone. The limestone goes into solution. The water carries it away.

With vinegar, we can see the reaction. Bubbles form. That's carbon dioxide. The limestone is being corroded. The floor collapses when the roof gives way. The groundwater level is falling. Drought helps. Pumping helps. Maize and sunflowers require a lot of water. Water leaves the cavities. The roof collapses. Then the ground collapses.

Level 2 · Standard reading

Imagine a maize field in central Anatolia, where the ground suddenly opened up, leaving a round hole with vertical sides. Around the town of Karapınar, in Konya Province, these cavities are called “obruks” and, in geology, collapse dolines. Researchers from Konya Technical University have identified around seven hundred sinkholes in the Konya closed basin, and six hundred and nineteen in the Karapınar area alone. Their diameter varies from three to sixty metres, and their depth from three to fifty metres.

A doline forms in limestone, a rock composed of calcium carbonate. Rain, which contains carbon dioxide, forms carbonic acid that slowly dissolves limestone, releasing calcium and bicarbonate ions. Over the course of millennia, the water carves out cracks and then proper cavities. The vinegar experiment shows the same reaction: when vinegar is poured onto limestone, bubbles of carbon dioxide immediately appear, whereas a quartz pebble does not react.

In the Konya Basin, the groundwater has declined by about 24 metres between one thousand nine hundred and eighty-three and two thousand and eight, eight metres of which over the past four years. Drought and intensive pumping, especially for maize and sunflower, exacerbate water loss, weakening the roof of cavities and causing them to collapse. Understanding these processes helps to protect people, avoid building on risk areas and better manage water resources.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

Imagine a corn field in central Anatolia, Turkey, where the ground suddenly opened, leaving a round hole with vertical walls. Around the town of Karapınar, these wells are called "obruks", dolines of collapse. Researchers at Konya Technical University recorded about seven hundred in the closed Konya basin, and only six hundred and nineteen in the Karapınar area. Their diameter varies from three to sixty metres and their depth ranges from three to fifty metres.

A doline is a closed depression, often circular, that forms in water-soluble rock, usually limestone. Calcareous limestone, composed of calcium carbonate, consists of shells, coral and chalk. A landscape carved by the dissolution of limestone is called karst.

The rain, charged with carbon dioxide, forms a low carbonic acid that attacks limestone. Calcium carbonate then transforms into calcium ions and hydrogenocarbonate ions, which remain dissolved in water. This very slow reaction hollows, for thousands of years, cracks and then underground cavities. The experience of vinegar shows the same rapid reaction: by pouring vinegar on limestone or chalk, bubbles of carbon dioxide appear immediately, while no reaction occurs with quartz or granite.

The soil collapses in Konya because the karst cavities, once sustained, lose their support when the groundwater drop. Near Karapınar, it has receded by about twenty-four metres between one thousand nine hundred eighty-three and two thousand eight, including eight metres in the last four years. Today, the fall is four to five meters a year, compared to a half-metre at the beginning of the two thousand years. Drought and excessive pumping, especially for maize and sunflowers, exacerbate this phenomenon.

Understanding dolines helps protect populations by avoiding building above fragile areas, optimizing water management and reducing pollution. The same acid-calcar reaction is found in everyday life, from vinegar that dissolves tartar to rain that erodes limestone statues, showing how a simple chemical reaction can reshape an entire landscape.

Key vocabulary

  • Dolines : Closed and circular depression forming in soluble rock.
  • limestone : Rock composed mainly of calcium carbonate.
  • karst : Landscape dug by the dissolution of limestone.
  • vinegar : Liquid containing ethanoic acid, more acid than rain.
  • Tablet : Groundwater tank that feeds the sources.
