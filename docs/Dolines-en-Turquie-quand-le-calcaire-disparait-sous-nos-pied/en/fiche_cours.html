Dolines in Turkey: when limestone disappears under our feet

In central Anatolia, near Karapınar, there are large circular holes called obruks, or dolines of collapse. These depressions are the result of a chemical reaction between water, carbon dioxide and limestone.

What is a doline?

A doline is a closed depression, more or less circular, that forms in a rock that water can dissolve.

Calcareous limestone, composed of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), is the most common rock; The landscapes dug by its dissolution form a karst.

To be remembered. A doline is a circular depression formed in limestone dissolved by water.

The chemistry of dissolution

The rain, through the soil, is charged with carbon dioxide and forms carbonic acid, a low acid.

This acid attacks calcium carbonate, transforming it into calcium ions and hydrogenocarbonate ions that remain dissolved in water, a very slow process that, over millennia, creates cracks, galleries and then cavities.

The equation is: calcium carbonate + water + carbon dioxide → calcium ions + two hydrogenocarbonate ions.

To be remembered. Carbonic acid transforms calcium carbonate into calcium and hydrogenocarbonate ions, which remain dissolved.
Be careful. Not to think that the rock disappears, it dissolves in the water.

The experience of vinegar

By pouring vinegar (ethanoic acid) on a piece of limestone or chalk, bubbles of carbon dioxide appear immediately, showing the reaction.

The same test on a quartz or granite scab produces no reaction, which shows that only rocks containing calcium carbonate react.

The equation with vinegar is: calcium carbonate + two ethanoic acid molecules → calcium ethanoate + water + carbon dioxide.

To be remembered. Ethanoic acid vinegar reacts rapidly with calcium carbonate by releasing CO2.
Be careful. Do not expect a reaction with quartz or granite.

Why the soil collapses

Under the Konya basin, karst rock has formed cavities over the millennia; the roof of these cavities lasts long before yielding.

The groundwater level dropped by about 24 metres between 1893 and 2008, of which eight metres in only the last four years, and today it decreases by four to five metres per year.

Two causes are accumulating: drought and excessive pumping for irrigation of corn and sunflower, often with illegal wells, which draw more water than nature restores.

When the water leaves the cavities, the roof is less supported and eventually collapses abruptly.

To be remembered. The drop in groundwater, aggravated by drought and excessive pumping, weakens the roofs of karst cavities, causing collapses.
Be careful. Do not neglect the human impact that accelerates the phenomenon.

When the doline becomes a monument

On the Turkish Mediterranean coast, near Silifke, the Dolines Cennet (Paradis) and Cehennem (Enfer) are famous: Cennet is about 250 m long and 70 m deep, with 452 steps leading to a small church of the 5th century.

Cehennem is narrower, with walls that dig inward, reaching a depth of 110 to 128 m, impossible to descend to the bottom.

These two sites are also collapsing dolines born in limestone and dug over thousands of years.

To be remembered. Some dolines, such as Cennet and Cehennem near Silifke, have become impressive tourist sites.

What's the point?

Understanding dolines helps protect populations by avoiding building roads, hangars or homes over fragile cavities, using maps of risk areas.

This knowledge helps to manage groundwater, which is essential for sources and aquifers that provide drinking water, while recalling that water flows quickly through cracks and is easily polluted.

In Konya, efficient irrigation methods such as dripping and pumping reduction help slow down the drop in the tablecloth, and the acid-calcary reaction is found daily in vinegar that removes tartar or rain that attacks limestone statues.

To be remembered. Understanding dolines protects people, manages water resources and avoids dangerous constructions.

What's the point?

This knowledge is primarily used to protect people by avoiding building on risk areas. It also helps to manage groundwater, essential for drinking water supply. Finally, it is useful in the fields of geology, spatial planning and agriculture, where karst risks must be taken into account.

Conclusion

Thus, a simple acid-calcium reaction can shape entire landscapes and influence our daily activities.
