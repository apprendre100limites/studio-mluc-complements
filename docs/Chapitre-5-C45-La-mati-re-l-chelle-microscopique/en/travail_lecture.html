Level 2 · standard reading

Hey! A glass of water seems smooth, but it is composed of particles so small that they are not seen. In a drop of water, there are more than a trillion billion molecules, a number difficult to imagine. The particulate model helps to understand how these small particles form the different states of matter.

In the solid, the particles are tight and tidy, each one only vibrate in its place. That's why the solid keeps its shape. In the liquid, the particles remain tight but slide over each other, so the liquid takes the shape of the container. In the gas, the particles are very distant, move quickly in all directions and leave a lot of vacuum between them, making the gas compressible.

Pressing the plunger of a syringe brings the particles closer to the gas without changing the number, which increases the pressure. The pressure comes from the particle shocks against the walls, and it increases when the temperature makes the particles move faster.

Dissolve sugar in water shows how solute particles slip between those of the solvent, forming a solution. To melt, dissolve or boil does not change molecules, only their organization. Thus, the particulate model allows engineers and mechanics to predict the behaviour of materials, from inflated tire to industrial metal. Observing the water or a bubble, we remember that it is the particles that constantly move.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

Hey! A glass of water seems smooth, but it consists of an infinity of invisible particles. In a drop, there are more than a trillion billion molecules, a number difficult to imagine. The particulate model, used by scientists, simplifies reality by representing matter as a set of small moving particles.

This model finds its roots in Robert Brown's observation, which, in one thousand eight hundred twenty-seven, sees tiny grains moving without being touched. He then understands that these grains are pushed from all sides by invisible water particles, a phenomenon called Brownian movement.

The particles explain the three states of matter. In a solid, they are tight and tidy, each one only vibrates in its place, giving the solid a clean shape. In a liquid, they remain close but slide on each other, so that the liquid takes the shape of the container. In a gas, the particles are very remote, move quickly in all directions and leave a lot of vacuum, filling all available space.

Because the gas contains a lot of emptiness, you can bring your particles together: the gas is compressible. By pressing the plunger of a syringe, the space between particles is reduced without changing the number. The pressure of a gas comes from the shocks of its particles against the walls; The more frequent the shocks, the higher the pressure, and a higher temperature makes the particles faster.

When sugar dissolves in water, its particles are inserted between water particles, forming a solution, a mixture of solvent and solute particles. Melting, boiling or dissolving are physical transformations: particles remain the same, only their organization changes. A water molecule remains the same, whether in ice or in steam.

This knowledge is useful in everyday life. Engineers create new materials by playing on particle organization, a mechanic inflates a tire by compressing a gas, and the particulate model allows predicting the behaviour of a material without having to test everything. For example, it has applications in industry, health and research.

In summary, the material is made of moving particles: tight and arranged in a solid, tight but moving in a liquid, distant in a gas. Changing state does not change particles, only their way of organizing. Think about those particles the next time you see a snout or a bubble.

Key vocabulary

  • solid : State of matter where particles are tight and arranged, each vibrates in its place.
  • Liquid : State where particles are tightened but slide over each other, taking the shape of the container.
  • gas : State where the particles are very far away, move quickly in all directions, with a lot of vacuum.
  • compression : Action to bring particles closer to a gas, reducing the space between them without changing their number.
  • Brownian movement : Random and constant movement of microscopic particles observed in a liquid.
