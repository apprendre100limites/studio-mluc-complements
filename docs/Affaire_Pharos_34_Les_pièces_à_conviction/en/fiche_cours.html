Pharos Case 3/4: The evidence

Imagine you are diving into the harbor of Alexandria, right next to a medieval fortress, the Qaitbay Fort. The water is murky.

A sunken site

Alexandria’s coastline has been sinking slowly since antiquity, a phenomenon called subsidence, which caused the location of the lighthouse at the tip of the Pharos peninsula to submerge beneath the Mediterranean along with its ruins. Today the underwater site covers about one and a half hectares, roughly the size of two football fields.

It consists mainly of blocks and fragments of hard stone, as well as lead, iron and bronze that were used to seal the stones. Since 1994, the Céalex team has been excavating the site one to two times per year, with each campaign’s length depending on weather and sea conditions. The team distinguishes the ruins of three different structures: the lighthouse to the northwest, a Roman building to the northeast, and a southern breakwater made of granite columns salvaged from the city. The lighthouse area is identified by the impressive size of its blocks and colossal statues, which can weigh up to forty tonnes.

Key points to remember. The sunken site of the Alexandria lighthouse spans one and a half hectares, contains massive blocks and three distinct groups of ruins, and has been studied since 1994 despite weather constraints.
Caution. Do not dive when the sea is rough, otherwise the campaign will be cancelled.

Photogrammetry

Divers used to lift blocks from the seabed using reference frames, direct and acoustic topography, which was time‑consuming and difficult.

Since 2012, photogrammetry involves taking many photos of the same object from all angles, each photo overlapping partially with the previous one.

A software identifies the same points across multiple photos, calculates their positions in space, and builds a three‑dimensional model, creating a digital twin that can be rotated, measured, and moved without touching the object.

As of January 1, 2020, the team had digitized about 65 % of the site and recorded 3 040 blocks in its database, of which 1 554 were already in digital twin form, making this site one of the largest submerged architecture sites in the world.

In June 2025, an exceptional campaign brought about twenty imposing blocks to the surface for study and digitization, allowing researchers to assemble them virtually like puzzle pieces.

Key points. Photogrammetry allows the creation of digital 3D models of underwater objects from many overlapping photos.
Caution. Ensure that the photos overlap sufficiently; otherwise the model will be incomplete.

Your mission

The main purpose of the specification sheet is to digitize an object.

The constraints are: take twenty to thirty photos, choose a stationary object, use soft light on a plain background and ensure the photos overlap.

Place a small matte and textured object (a stone, a shell or a figurine) on a support in front of a plain background, lit softly without harsh shadows, then rotate around and photograph from several heights while keeping the object stable.

Then, using the software indicated by the teacher, create the digital twin and note in the investigation notebook what worked well and what caused problems (blurred area, hole in the model, shadow).

This procedure reproduces the difficulties encountered by underwater archaeologists.

Key points. To digitize a small object, take 20‑30 photos that overlap, with soft light and a plain background, then create the 3D model and record the strengths and problems.
Caution. Do not move the object during shooting, otherwise the model will be incorrect.

What is it used for?

Photogrammetry is used to draw maps and plans, preserve heritage, create sets for video games and films, and inspect buildings without climbing them.

Professions that use this technique include diving archaeologist, surveyor, land surveyor and 3D digitization specialist, whether underwater or on land, to keep a precise record of what exists.

Key points. Photogrammetry is useful for mapping, heritage preservation and the creation of digital sets, and is used by many professions such as archaeologists and topographers.

What's the point?

Photogrammetry makes it possible to draw maps and plans, preserve heritage and create scenery for video games and movies. It is used in everyday life to control buildings without going up there and to document historical sites. The professions that need it are the diver archaeologist, the topographer, the geometer and the specialist in 3D digitization.

Conclusion

Thus, photogrammetry opens up new possibilities for exploring and preserving underwater treasures.
