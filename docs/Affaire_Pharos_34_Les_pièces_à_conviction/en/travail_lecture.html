Level 3 · in-depth reading

Imagine diving into the port of Alexandria, near the fort of Qaitbay. The water is cloudy and on the bottom you see huge blocks of stone, pieces of columns and fragments of statues. You don't look at a bunch of rocks, but the place where the lighthouse stood.

This lighthouse is underwater because the coast of Alexandria has been sinking slowly since ancient times. Researchers call it subsidence. The location of the lighthouse, at the eastern tip of the peninsula of Pharos, has thus plunged under the Mediterranean, with the ruins it carried. Today, this underwater site covers about a hectare and a half, about two football fields.

The Céalex team searches there once or twice a year, since one thousand nine hundred and ninety-four. The duration of each campaign depends on the weather and sea condition. When the sea is bad, you don't dive. The team distinguishes the ruins of three different buildings. The lighthouse, northwest of the site. A construction of Roman times, to the northeast. And a dike, to the south, that protects the coast and is made of granite columns recovered in the city. The lighthouse area recognizes the impressive size of its colossal blocks and statues, which can weigh up to 40 tons.

Photogrammetry changes everything. This method allows us to study thousands of blocks of stone laid at the bottom of the water, without being able to remove them each time. How? We take many photos of the same object from all angles, making sure that each photo partially covers the previous one. A software identifies the same points on several photos, calculates their position in space, and builds a three-dimensional model. In the end, you get a digital double of the object, which you can rotate, measure and move without ever touching it.

By 1 January two thousand and twenty, the team had digitized the surface of about sixty-five percent of the site, and recorded three thousand and forty blocks in its database. One hundred and fifty-four blocks or fragments already existed in digital doubles. It is one of the world's largest engulfed architectural sites. In June, two thousand and twenty-five, an exceptional campaign took a new stage: some twenty of the largest blocks were raised to the surface to be studied and digitized. Researchers can now try them against each other on a computer, like the pieces of a puzzle.

Your mission is to digitize an object. Choose a small object, like a stone, a shell, a figurine: something matte, with relief, and without reflections. Put it on a stand, in front of a united background, with soft light, without hard shadows. Then turn around and take your photos, under several heights. Each photo must show much of what the previous one showed. Attention: the object must not move during shooting, only you. Then, with the software chosen by your teacher, create your digital double. In your survey book, note what worked well, and what was a problem: a blurred area, a hole in the model, a shadow. This is exactly what archaeologists meet.

Photogrammetry is used to draw maps and plans, preserve heritage, make video games and film sets, and control buildings without going up there. On the trades side, we find the archaeologist diver, the topographer, the geometer, the specialist in three-dimensional digitization. Under water and on land, these trades are used to keep a precise record of what exists.

Key vocabulary

  • Subsidence : Progressive melting of the coast of Alexandria since ancient times.
  • photogrammetry : Method that creates a 3D model from many photos of an object.
  • block : Large piece of hard stone found on the underwater site.
  • dike : Construction south of the site that protects the coast, made of granite columns.
  • Double digital : 3D virtual representation of a real object that can be manipulated without touching it.
