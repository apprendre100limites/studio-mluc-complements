Level 2 · standard reading

A sound is a vibration, a fast going-and-coming particle. A guitar string, your vocal cords, the membrane of a speaker: all vibrate. An object that vibrates and produces a sound is called a sound source. You can feel it yourself. Put two fingers on your throat and say "aaaah" out loud: you feel your vocal cords vibrate.

The sound spreads from close to close. Without particles, no sound. In the void, the sound does not spread. It needs a material medium: air, water or a solid. The sound goes faster in water than in air, and even faster in steel. In the air, at twenty degrees, it advances to about three hundred and forty meters per second. An example with the storm. You see the lightning, then you count five seconds before you hear the thunder. The light comes almost instantly, but not the sound: it has travelled about a thousand seven hundred meters.

The ear is a sound receptor: it receives vibrations and turns them into signals that your brain understands. A microphone does the same thing, but it produces electrical variations. The sound level is measured in decibels, with a sonometer. A whisper makes about thirty decibels, a conversation sixty, a dense circulation eighty, and a concert one hundred to one hundred ten. From about eighty-five decibels, prolonged exposure can permanently damage your hearing.

To listen to safe music: get away from the speakers, because the sound level drops with distance. Take regular breaks. Limit the volume of your helmet. And in concert, put some plugs on. A full helmet is comfortable at the moment. But your ear does not repair: a damage caused by the noise is definitive.

A sound is a vibration. It spreads in a material environment, never in a vacuum. Its level is measured in decibels, and from eighty-five decibels it is necessary to protect its ears. A sound can be used in health, music, cinema, safety at work and boat sonars.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

A sound is a vibration: a very fast going-and-coming particles of matter. When a guitar rope, your vocal cords or the membrane of a speaker starts to vibrate, we talk about a sound source. You can even feel your vocal cords by putting two fingers on your throat and saying "aaaah" out loud.

Vibration is transmitted from close to close: one particle moves its neighbour, which puts another in motion. What travels is vibration, not matter. Without particles, there's no sound. In the void, the sound does not spread. It needs a material medium: air, water or solid. In the air, at twenty degrees, it advances to about three hundred and forty meters per second. A simple example is thunderstorm: you see lightning, then you count five seconds before hearing thunder. The light comes almost instantly, but not the sound, which has travelled about a thousand seven hundred meters.

The ear is a sound receiver: it receives vibrations and turns them into signals that your brain understands. A microphone does the same thing, but it produces electrical variations. The sound level is measured in decibels, with a sonometer. A whisper makes about thirty decibels, a conversation sixty, a dense circulation eighty, and a concert one hundred to one hundred ten. From about eighty-five decibels, prolonged exposure can permanently damage your hearing.

To listen to music safely, get away from the speakers, because the sound level drops with distance. Take regular breaks. Limit the volume of your helmet. And in concert, put some plugs on. A helmet is comfortable at the moment, but your ear does not repair: a damage caused by noise is definitive.

These concepts are used in health, music, cinema, safety at work and boat sonars. An audioprosthetist equips deaf or hard of hearing people. A sound engineer records and regulates the volume of concerts. An ENT doctor treats hearing. And an ultrasound artist uses ultrasound to see inside the body. To sum up: a sound is a vibration, it spreads in a material medium, never in a vacuum. Its level is measured in decibels, and from eighty-five decibels it is necessary to protect its ears. In space, why can't anyone hear you screaming? Because there is no matter to transmit the sound: the sound does not spread in the void. Next time you hear a thunder, count the seconds: you can now calculate the distance.
