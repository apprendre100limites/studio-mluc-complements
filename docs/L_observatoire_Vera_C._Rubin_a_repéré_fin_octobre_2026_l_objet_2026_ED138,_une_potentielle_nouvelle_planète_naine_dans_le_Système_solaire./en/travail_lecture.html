Level 2 · standard reading

You may have heard of a new object spotted at the edge of the Solar System, named two thousand twenty‑six ED138. Astronomers think it could become a dwarf planet, but first several criteria need to be verified.

A dwarf planet is a body that revolves around the Sun like planets, but is smaller and shares its orbit with other objects. To be recognized, it must meet three conditions: to rotate around the Sun, to be massive enough for its gravity to make it spherical, and not to have cleaned the surroundings of its orbit. Pluto, since two thousand and six, clearly illustrates this type of object.

The object was discovered in data from Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile. The astronomer Matthew Holman spotted on 13 October two thousand twenty-six, and the announcement was made on 27 October. The Rubin telescope had already photographed thirty times between March and July two thousand twenty-six, and it also appears on images dating from two thousand sixteen. His orbit places him at about forty-six astronomical units of the Sun, hence further than Pluto.

It remains to be measured precisely its size, estimated between six hundred and one thousand five hundred kilometers, to know whether it is large enough to be round. Infrared observations, for example with the James Webb telescope, are required. Its orbit is inclined about twenty-five degrees, and scientists suspect Neptune's influence.

In short, two thousand twenty-six ED138 meets the first two criteria of a dwarf planet, but its shape still needs to be confirmed before joining Pluto among the dwarf planets.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

In the video, we first explain what a dwarf planet is. It is a body that revolves around the Sun like the great planets, but is smaller and shares its orbit with other objects. To be classified as such, three criteria must be met: go around the Sun, be massive enough for its gravity to make it spherical, and not have "cleaned" its orbital vicinity. The gravity of an object large enough shapes it into a ball, while a small rock remains irregular. The eight planets of the solar system pass the three tests, while the dwarf planets fail only the third. Pluto, since two thousand and six, illustrates this case.

Then, the video describes how astronomers discovered the object named two thousand twenty-six ED138. The images come from Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, whose telescope photographes the sky every night. Astronomer Matthew Holman of Harvard and Smithsonian dastrophysics Centre spotted the body on 13 October two thousand twenty-six, and the announcement took place on 27 October. The observatory had already captured the object thirty times between March and July of the same year, and it is even found in photographs dating from two thousand and sixteen. These multiple photographs allowed us to track its displacement and calculate its orbit, which is about forty-six astronomical units of the Sun, thus further than Pluto on average.

The third part shows what remains to be verified. Its size is estimated between six hundred and one thousand five hundred kilometers, a very wide range. Without a precise measure, it is not known whether the gravity is sufficient to make it spherical. Observations in the infrared, for example with the James Webb Space Telescope, are required. Its orbit is inclined about twenty-five degrees from the planet plane, and scientists believe that Neptune could play a role in this inclination. Thus, two thousand twenty-six ED138 remains a candidate: if it proves spherical, it will join Pluto among the dwarf planets.

Key vocabulary

  • dwarf planet : A star that revolves around the Sun, smaller than a planet and has not cleared its orbit.
  • orbit : The path a body follows around the Sun.
  • gravity : Force that pulls objects toward the center of a body.
  • astronomical unit : Average distance between Earth and the Sun.
  • James Webb space telescope : Telescope placed in space that observes in the infrared.
