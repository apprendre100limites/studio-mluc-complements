From the ocean to kerosene

Instruction. Choose the reading level that suits you. Read the text, then note the main points in the column on the right.

Level 1 · simplified reading

The project aims to convert seawater into fuel. The researchers want to produce kerosene directly on the ocean. The aim is to refuel the fighter jets on aircraft carriers. Currently, these aircraft are refuelled by tankers. This supply chain is cumbersome and vulnerable. Producing fuel at sea would make the ship self-sufficient. The process takes place in two stages. First, the carbon and hydrogen dissolved in water are extracted. A special filter called the ECEM module captures these gases with an efficiency of 92%. The gases are then recombined using the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. This reaction assembles atoms like Lego bricks. The final product is pure aviation kerosene. The system requires a lot of energy. It requires about 200 MW continuously. This power output is equivalent to that of an aircraft carrier's nuclear reactor. Scientists use molybdenum carbide catalysts. If everything works, we will be able to produce nearly 310,000 litres of fuel every day. This could change the way planes and ships refuel.

Level 2 · standard reading

In the near future, researchers are working on an innovation that could revolutionise the energy industry. Their goal is to extract aviation kerosene directly from seawater, which seems like something out of a science fiction film. Large ships, such as aircraft carriers, are currently powered by nuclear reactors, which allow them to sail for decades without refuelling. However, the fighter jets on their decks are very fuel-hungry and need to be refuelled frequently.

To solve this problem, scientists have developed a two-step process. First, they extract carbon dioxide and hydrogen from seawater. Then they recombine them chemically to create kerosene. This process uses an ultra-technological filter, the ECEM module, which captures these gases with an efficiency of 92%.

The second challenge is to make fuel. This requires extreme precision and a great amount of energy. To produce 310,000 litres of kerosene per day, huge volumes of water must be processed. Scientists have developed salt-resistant catalysts to survive ocean corrosion.

If these challenges are overcome, the impact could be global. This technology could be used for civil aviation and cargo, forming a closed carbon cycle. This could help to move away from polluting fossil fuels. The question then arises: could seawater become tomorrow's oil?

Before assembly, the CO₂ must be converted into carbon monoxide, a crucial step to avoid the unnecessary production of methane.\n\nThe technical challenge is significant. Producing around 310,000 litres of fuel each day requires 200 MW of electricity, equivalent to the power of a small urban grid, which an aircraft carrier's nuclear reactor can provide. In addition, huge volumes of water must be filtered continuously while resisting salt corrosion thanks to molybdenum carbide catalysts, which are cheaper than rare metals.\n\nIf these obstacles are overcome, the technology could be extended to civil aviation and cargo ships, creating a closed carbon cycle and reducing dependence on fossil fuels. The ocean would then become an inexhaustible source of energy, paving the way for a new energy era."

Key vocabulary

  • fuel : Liquid substance used to power aircraft.
  • corrosion : Degradation of equipment due to the action of marine salt.
  • synthesis : Chemical process that recombines the extracted gases to form a hydrocarbon.
  • energy : Power required to operate the transformation process.
  • hydrogen : Gases present in seawater, used as the base brick of the fuel.
