From the ocean to kerosene

This concept aims to turn ocean water into fuel. It is presented as a scientific reality and a technological adventure that pushes the limits of what is possible.

From the idea to the wild project

Seawater is not just water and salt. It contains carbon dioxide and hydrogen, which can serve as building blocks for a fuel.

The project aims to extract aviation kerosene directly from the ocean.

Key point. Turning seawater into kerosene is a scientific challenge.
Caution. Do not assume that this makes a simple fuel.

The logistical problem of aircraft carriers

Giant aircraft carriers are powered by nuclear reactors, but their aircraft do not run on nuclear energy.

These aircraft have a thirst for liquid fuel and burn thousands of litres each hour of flight.

Key point. Aircraft on carriers need to be refueled with fuel.
Caution. Forget that the reactors are not used for the aircraft.

The two‑step process

First, gases are extracted from seawater with an ECEM module that captures 92 % of the gases.

Then, carbon and hydrogen are recombined to produce a liquid hydrocarbon, aviation kerosene.

Key point. The Fischer‑Tropsch synthesis assembles atoms to make kerosene.
Caution. Assuming that carbon dioxide can be used as‑is.

Technical challenges

The process requires phenomenal energy, continuous pumping and filtering of water, and resists oceanic corrosion.

To produce about 310,000 liters of fuel per day, 200 MW of electricity is needed continuously.

Take note. Overcoming these challenges is necessary for large‑scale use.
Caution. Do not underestimate the required energy and filtering.

Towards a possible revolution.

If the technology is mastered, the ocean could become a power source for ships and civil aviation.

This could create a large closed carbon cycle and move away from polluting fossil fuels.

Take note. Sea water could become tomorrow's oil.
Caution. Thinking that the technology is already ready for use.

What is it for?

This technology is used to produce fuel for aircraft on aircraft carriers. It could serve civil aviation and cargo ships. It is needed for large fleets and in the fields of chemistry, naval engineering, and research.

Conclusion.

The conversion of seawater into kerosene is therefore a fascinating project for future energy.
