Sound: from vibrations to medicine

Consign. Choose the reading level that suits you. Read the text, then note the main points in the column on the right.

Level 1 · simplified reading

Sound is a vibration that spreads into the air. Everything we hear comes from something moving. Speak and put two fingers on your throat: you feel this tremor that becomes the sound. This vibration is transmitted from one molecule to another as a wave. A sound needs a medium to move, no air, no water, no metal, and the wave cannot travel. In the air, the sound moves three hundred and forty meters per second. The tighter the molecules, the faster the sound moves. A sound has two characteristics: its frequency, which gives the height, and its amplitude, which gives the volume. A high frequency gives an acute sound, a low frequency gives a serious sound. The amplitude gives the strength of the sound. Infrasounds are sounds below twenty hertz, ultrasounds are above twenty thousand hertz. An echo is a sound that bounces over an obstacle.

Mickael Tanter, a French physicist, uses ultrasound to create images of the human body. He developed an ultrafast ultrasound technique that produces thousands of images per second. This technology helps diagnose brain diseases. He then worked on the heart, especially the aortic shrinkage. His technique is to send ultrasound to soften the sick tissue without cutting. After clinical trials, this technique has been internationally recognized. The start-up CardiaWave was created to market this technique. It allows the heart to be treated without opening it, thus reducing the risk to the patient and the length of hospitalisation.

Level 2 · standard reading

The sound comes from a vibration: a rope that trembles, a membrane that moves forward and backwards, even your vocal cords. Without this little movement, nothing can ring. Vibration is transmitted from molecule to molecule, like a wave that travels through the sea, and becomes a wave that needs a material medium to travel. In the air, the sound runs three hundred and forty meters per second, much slower than the light that goes eight hundred and eighty thousand times faster, hence the lightning that one sees before thunder. In water it reaches a thousand five hundred meters per second, and in steel five thousand: the tighter the molecules, the faster the wave moves.

Two greatnesses describe a sound. Its frequency, measured in hertz, gives the height: a high frequency produces an acute sound, a low frequency a serious sound. Its amplitude, measured in decibels, determines the volume and can damage the ears. The more mysterious stamp recognizes a piano and a guitar even when they play the same note. The human ear only hears between twenty and twenty thousand hertz. Below, infrasounds are used for elephants, above, ultrasound helps bats locate themselves.

Mickael Tanter exploited these invisible ultrasounds to create an ultrafast ultrasound that produces thousands of images every second, much more than the 50 classic images. This technique made it possible to film blood in the brain without opening the skull.

He then imagined a virtual bistouri to treat aortic shrinkage. After 15 years of research, a clinical trial of 40 patients showed that focused ultrasonic waves could soften the valve without cutting. The results published in two thousand twenty-three led to the creation of the start-up CardiaWave, which received the CE mark to care for the heart in Europe.

In summary, three words sum up everything: vibration, medium, frequency. From simple middle‑school physics today emerges a tool capable of repairing hearts.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

The sound is born of a vibration: a rope that trembles, a membrane that moves forward and backwards, our vocal cords that shake. Without this little movement, no waves are created and nothing can travel. Vibration is transmitted from molecule to molecule, like a wave that travels through the sea, and so information moves in the air. She needs a material environment; Without air, water, or metal, there was nothing left to spread, hence the silence of space. In the air, the sound runs three hundred and forty meters per second, much slower than the light, which explains why one sees lightning before hearing thunder. Its speed varies according to the medium: one thousand five hundred meters per second in water, five thousand in steel, because the tighter the molecules, the faster the wave moves.

Two sizes describe the sound: frequency, which gives the height (acute or severe), and amplitude, which gives the volume and can damage the ears. The more subtle stamp recognizes a piano and a guitar even when they play the same note. Our ear perceives only between twenty and twenty thousand hertz; below, infrasounds are used by elephants, above, ultrasounds are used by bats to orientate.

Mickael Tanter used these inaudible ultrasounds to create an ultrafast ultrasound, capable of producing thousands of images per second. This advances the ability to film the blood that circulates in the brain in real time, without opening the body, without radiation and without contrast. By pushing further, he imagined a "virtual bistouri": focused ultrasonic waves that soften a narrowed heart valve, thus avoiding invasive surgery. The first trials on forty patients showed the safety of the technique, paving the way for less risky treatments, shorter stays and faster recovery.

Thus, from simple vibration to modern medicine, sound shows that middle school physics can become a tool that heals hearts.

Key vocabulary

  • vibration : Rapid movement of an object that creates a sound.
  • wave : Transmission of vibration from one molecule to another like a wave.
  • frequency : Number of vibrations per second, measured in hertz, which determines the pitch of the sound.
  • amplitude : Amplitude of the back-and-forth motion that determines the sound’s volume.
  • stamp : Sound quality that allows the instrument that produces it to be recognized.
