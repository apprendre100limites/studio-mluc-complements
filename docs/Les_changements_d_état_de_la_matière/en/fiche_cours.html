Changes in the state of matter

Around you, matter changes state all the time: a melting ice cube, boiling water or fog on a mirror. In this course, you'll learn the name of each state change, what a temperature range is, and why water doesn't boil at a hundred degrees everywhere.

State changes

The transition from solid to liquid is called fusion. From liquid to gas, it's vaporization. From gas to liquid, it is liquefaction. And from liquid to solid, it's solidification.

An ice cube that melts in a glass is a melt: at normal pressure, the ice melts at zero degrees Celsius. The mist on a bathroom mirror is a liquefaction: the water vapor becomes liquid again on contact with a cold surface.

Takeaway. There are four state changes: melting, vaporization, liquefaction, and solidification.

The boiling of a pure body

When pure water is heated, its temperature rises and then stops at one hundred degrees Celsius. Vapor bubbles form throughout the liquid: this is boiling. As long as there is liquid, the temperature does not move.

This stable temperature during the change of state is called a temperature plateau. Evaporation is different: it is slow, takes place only on the surface and can take place at any temperature.

Takeaway. For a pure body like water, the temperature remains stable during boiling: this is the temperature plateau.
Caution. Do not confuse evaporation and boiling: evaporation occurs only at the surface, at any temperature, while boiling occurs throughout the liquid, at a precise temperature.

Cooling a pure liquid

When liquid water is cooled, its temperature drops to zero degrees, and then it remains stable as ice forms. This is a new level: solidification.

For a pure body, melting and solidification take place at the same temperature. For water, it is zero degrees.

Takeaway. When the water solidifies, the temperature remains at zero degrees as long as the ice forms.

What about blends?

With a mixture, like salt water, there is no plateau: the temperature continues to rise during boiling.

This is a good purity test. If the temperature stabilizes during the change of state, the liquid is a pure body. Otherwise, it's a mixture.

Takeaway. A mixture like salt water has no temperature plateau: its temperature continues to rise during boiling.

Boiling and pressure

Air exerts pressure on us: atmospheric pressure, measured with a barometer. At sea level, it is worth about one thousand and thirteen hectopascals. The higher we climb, the thinner the layer of air above us, so the pressure decreases.

The boiling temperature of water decreases with pressure. At the top of Mont Blanc, at about four thousand eight hundred meters, the water boils at about eighty-four degrees. In a pressure cooker, the pressure is greater: the water boils above one hundred degrees, around one hundred and twenty, and the food cooks faster.

Takeaway. When the pressure decreases, as in altitude, the water boils at a lower temperature; when the pressure increases, as in a pressure cooker, it boils at a higher temperature.

What's the point?

Refrigerator and air conditioning cold comes from state changes, and in a thermal power plant, water becomes steam to produce electricity. We need it in everyday life to get cold, cool a room or cook faster in a pressure cooker. Among the trades concerned are the refrigerator, the plant technician and the meteorologist.

Conclusion

A pure body has a temperature plateau, a mixture does not, and the water boils at a hundred degrees at sea level but at a lower temperature at altitude.
