Level 2 · Standard reading

In any investigation, there are witnesses. Some spoke more than two thousand years ago, others nine hundred years ago. Our job is to listen to them, compare them, and paint a sketch of Alexandria's lighthouse.

Strabon, a Greek geographer, visited Egypt about twenty-five BC. He describes a multi-storey white stone tower. The coins struck in Alexandria, under the Domitian emperors to Commode, engrave the lighthouse on discs of twenty-five to thirty-five millimetres.

Ibn al-Shaykh of Malaga spent several months in Alexandria in a thousand one hundred and sixty-five and one thousand sixty-six. He climbed floor after floor with a graduated rope, a leaf and a feather, measuring each corner. The researchers regard his story as the most accurate and complete.

A riddle persists: tradition places a fire at the top, while currencies show a large statue at the top. Under water, archaeologists found blocks of granite, quartzite, marble and limestone, some weighing up to forty tons.

Your mission, presented as a specification, is to model the lighthouse in three dimensions. Only three volumes: a pavement, an eight-sided prism and a cylinder. Respect the scale provided, follow the testimonies and justify each piece.

Three-dimensional modelling is used to verify that a building, bridge or car is assembled before construction. These occupations exist: architect, draftsman, engineer, maquettist. In the next episode, we dive into the exhibits.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

In each historical investigation, the witnesses are the pieces of the puzzle that help to reconstruct the past. The lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the wonders of the ancient world, is studied through four very different witnesses, each providing precise and complementary elements. The first, Strabon, a Greek geographer, describes the lighthouse as a multi-storey white stone tower, described as "admirable". His account is short, but he confirms the existence of a monumental construction from the time of Hellenistic kingdoms. The second witness is the series of coins struck at the Alexandria workshop between Emperor Domitian and Emperor Commode. On these small discs you can see a solid base, a large ramp in degrees supported by arcades, a wide door, then three successive sections: square with windows and cornice, octagonal and finally round, surmounted by a statue. The engravers, forced to hold everything on a disc, keep only the essential, but they offer an overview of the lighthouse plan. The third witness comes from architectural copies discovered in Taposiris Magna, about 40 kilometers to the west, and from a terracotta lantern from Fayoum. These reduced models accurately replicate the three parts of the Great Lighthouse, confirming that this pattern was a recurring model. The last witness, Ibn al-Shaykh, a scientist from Malaga, climbed into the lighthouse in one thousand one hundred sixty-five and one thousand sixty-six, measuring each corner with a graded rope. His report states that the first section was made of cut stones, accessible by a ramp, that the second section was made of bricks and plaster with a staircase, and that the last section was round, with a staircase leading to a terrace covered with a wooden dome.

These sources are assessed on three questions: did the witness see, measured, or simply copied. Thus, Strabon's narratives and the images of the rooms offer an overview, the copies confirm the architectural pattern, and the measurements of Ibn al-Shaykh give the most reliable detail. By crossing this information, researchers can reconstruct the portrait-robot of the lighthouse, understand why each testimony is essential and prepare the 3D modeling that will be used to visualize this missing monument.
