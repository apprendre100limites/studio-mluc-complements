Pharos Case 2/4: The composite sketch

In each investigation, witnesses from different periods tell us about the Lighthouse of Alexandria. Our task is to listen to them, compare them, and create its composite sketch.

The four witnesses

Four different witnesses describe the lighthouse: Strabo the Greek geographer, the coins from the Alexandria workshop, copies of small lighthouses, and Ibn al‑Shaykh the scholar from Malaga.

Strabo, who visited Egypt around 25 BC, describes the lighthouse as a multi‑storey tower of white stone, using a Greek word meaning “admirably”; his testimony is precise but very brief.

Coins minted between 91 and 188 AD show a solid base, a large stepped ramp supported by arcades, a first square section with windows and a cornice, triton statues at the four corners, then a second multi‑sided section, a third circular section, and at the top a statue; everything is reduced to fit on a disc of 25‑35 mm.

At Taposiris Magna, about forty kilometers west, a small 2nd‑century BC lighthouse reproduces the same three‑part scheme (square, octagonal, circular); a terracotta lantern from Fayoum shows the same three parts, serving as a scale model of the great lighthouse.

Ibn al‑Shaykh, in 1265‑66, climbs the lighthouse with a graduated rope, a sheet and a feather, and measures every corner; he describes the first section in cut stone with a ramp, the second in bricks and plaster with a staircase, and the last round with a staircase leading to a small terrace covered by a wooden dome.

Not all witnesses are equal: one wonders whether they saw the lighthouse, measured it, or simply copied from other sources.

Key points. The four witnesses – Strabo, the pieces, the copies, and Ibn al‑Shaykh – enable the lighthouse to be reconstructed, but their reliability must be assessed by asking whether they saw it, measured it, or simply copied it.
Caution. Do not consider all witnesses as equally reliable; you must verify whether they actually observed the lighthouse.

The mystery of the fire and the block puzzle.

Tradition places a fire at the very top of the lighthouse, but the coins show a large statue at the summit, making it difficult to imagine that the fire and the statue share the same spot.

We do not know exactly where the fire was located or what role the mirror mentioned by the Arabic tradition played; no one knows for certain.

At the bottom of the water, archaeologists found blocks of granite, quartzite, marble and limestone, some weighing up to forty tonnes, as well as pieces of lead, iron and bronze used to seal them together.

From these blocks, researchers reconstructed monumental gates, creating a giant puzzle where each block must be studied and then placed relative to the others.

Key points. The location of the fire and the function of the mirror remain unknown, while the recovered blocks allow the lighthouse to be reconstructed as a giant puzzle.
Attention. Do not assume that the fire and the statue occupied the same place without evidence.

Your mission.

Main objective of the mission: model the lighthouse in 3D.

Constraints: only three volumes (a cube, an octagonal prism, and a cylinder), respect the given scale, follow the testimonies, and justify each placed piece.

First part: using the testimonies, sketch the lighthouse’s robotic portrait in these three volumes, then model it in a free software such as Tinkercad, with the teacher providing dimensions and scale.

Second part: with the blocks and the group’s clue cards, assemble a stable structure and justify the position of each piece.

Key points. The mission is to create a 3D model of the lighthouse with three geometric shapes, then assemble the discovered blocks, justifying each placement.
Attention. Avoid ignoring the scale or shape constraints imposed by the testimonies.

What is it for?

Before building a building, a bridge, or a car, we first draw it in three dimensions at the precise scale to verify that everything fits together.

These skills are used in the professions of architect, designer, engineer and model maker.

Archaeologists apply the same method in reverse: they start from the pieces found to reconstruct what has disappeared.

Takeaway. 3D modeling is used to predict the assembly of technical projects and helps archaeologists to reconstruct missing monuments.

What's the point?

3D modeling makes it possible to visualize and verify the assembly of a project before its construction. It is used in everyday life to design buildings, bridges or vehicles. The trades that use it are architect, designer, engineer, model maker, and archaeologists apply it backwards to reconstruct the past.

Conclusion

By combining the testimonies, the block puzzle and the 3D modeling, we are approaching the reconstruction of the lighthouse of Alexandria.
