Level 2 · Standard reading

Syrup in water, oil in vinegar: some mixtures are done without problems, others refuse to mix. Why does sugar disappear in your tea, while oil stays separate from water? That is what we will understand.

Dissolution is a common phenomenon. You leave a sugar in a glass of water and you move: gradually, it disappears. Sugar is the solute, water is the solvent and the mixture obtained is the solution. A solution is a homogeneous mixture: with the naked eye, you see only one liquid.

Is there a limit to dissolution? If you keep adding sugar to your tea, there comes a time when it stays at the bottom of the glass, and it does not dissolve. There is a maximum solute mass that can be dissolved in a litre of solvent. We call it solubility. When this limit is reached, the solution is saturated.

Two liquids may be miscible or non-miscible. When you mix water and mint syrup, you get a single liquid: a homogeneous mixture. They say these liquids are miscible. Water and alcohol are too. With water and oil, you see two layers: it's a heterogeneous mixture. These liquids are unmiscible. The oil floats above, because it is less dense.

To recognize a mixture, look at how many phases you see. Only one is a homogeneous mixture. Many are a heterogeneous mixture. And if something is dissolved, identify the solute and solvent.

What's all this for? Not just to pass control. A pharmacist prepares solutions whose amount of solute must be accurate. A painter chooses his solvent: water for a water painting, a diluent for an oil painting. And on an aquaculture farm, we monitor the salt dissolved in the pond water. These concepts are used in health, construction, agriculture and research.

In short: the solute dissolves in the solvent and gives a solution. Solubility is the limit of what can be dissolved. Two liquids are miscible or non-miscible.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

Hey! When you put sugar in your tea and stir, sugar gradually disappears. Sugar is the solute, i.e. the dissolved substance, and tea water is the solvent, the liquid that does it. The mixture obtained, sweet water, is a homogeneous solution: with the naked eye one sees only one liquid even if several substances are present.

However, there is a limit to what can be dissolved. If you keep adding sugar, he'll eventually stay at the bottom of the glass. This limit is called solubility and is expressed in grams per litre. In water at 20 degrees, cooking salt has a solubility of about three hundred and sixty grams per litre, while sugar salt is much larger, nearly two kilograms per litre. Thus, 50 grams of salt can be dissolved in half a litre of water, as the limit for half a litre is one hundred and eighty grams, so the sugar or salt remains dissolved until the quantity exceeds that value.

When mixing two liquids, the result depends on their ability to combine. Water and mint syrup form a single homogeneous liquid; They're said to be miscible. Water and oil, on the other hand, form two distinct layers, a heterogeneous mixture, because the oil is less dense and does not mix with water. If you observe a single phase, the liquids are miscible; If you see many, they're not miscible.

These concepts are useful in many areas. A pharmacist prepares solutions where the amount of solute must be accurate, a painter chooses his solvent according to the type of paint, and in an aquaculture farm the amount of salt dissolved in the water of the basins is controlled. Understanding dissolution, solubility and miscibility therefore solves concrete problems in health, construction, agriculture and research.

In summary, the solute dissolves in the solvent to form a solution, solubility determines the maximum amount that can be dissolved, and two liquids are miscible or not depending on whether they form a single liquid or several layers. Now, when you prepare your tea, you know exactly who is the solute, who is the solvent and why the mixture appears as it is.

Key vocabulary

  • solute : substance dissolved in liquid
  • solvent : liquid that dissolves the solute
  • solution : homogeneous mixture obtained after dissolution
  • miscible : which mixes with another liquid to form a homogeneous mixture
  • solubility : maximum mass of solute that can dissolve in a litre of solvent
