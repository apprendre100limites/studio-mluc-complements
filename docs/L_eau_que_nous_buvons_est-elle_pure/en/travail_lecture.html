Is the water we drink pure?

Consign. Choose the level of reading that suits you. Read the text, then note the essentials in the right column.

Level 1 · Simplified reading

The water you drink contains water, salts and gases. She's not pure. To prove that there is water, anhydrous copper sulphate can be used. If the powder turns blue, there's water.

Mineral water contains salts. If we heat mineral water, we see white deposits that remain. That's what was dissolved in the water.

In sparkling water, there's gas. You can see it appearing by forming bubbles when you open the bottle. To detect this gas, lime water can be used that becomes white.

Clean water is neutral. It contains almost nothing. Tap water and mineral water are mixtures.

Level 2 · Standard reading

The water we drink every day is not just water. It's a mixture of water with salts and gases. To prove the presence of water, anhydrous copper sulphate can be used that becomes blue in contact with water. Dissolved salts can be recovered by heating water until it evaporates, leaving a deposit at the bottom. Seawater contains about thirty-five grams of dissolved salt per litre. Dissolved gases, such as carbon dioxide, can be detected with disturbed lime water. Minerals such as calcium, magnesium and sodium are found in mineral water. Distilled water obtained by distillation is the purest but not intended for regular consumption. Pure water, which is pH neutral, is almost pure and contains only water. However, the water we drink is a mixture of water, salts and gas.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

The water we drink every day is not just pure water. It is a mixture containing water, salts and gases.

To understand what water contains, we can use simple tests. For example, if we add anhydrous copper sulphate to a liquid, the powder becomes blue if the liquid contains water. However, this powder is toxic, so use it only under the supervision of your teacher.

Salts are also present in water. If we heat mineral water, the water turns into steam, leaving behind a white deposit, i.e. dissolved salts. In seawater, there are about thirty-five grams of salt per litre. In salt marshes, water is allowed to evaporate to recover salt. On a bottle of mineral water, the label indicates dissolved minerals: calcium, magnesium, sodium. It also notes the dry residue, which is what remains when all the water has evaporated.

Gases are also present in water. In sparkling water, we see bubbles appearing, it's carbon dioxide. To detect this gas, lime water is used. If we pass it through carbon dioxide, it becomes cloudy, white and milky. Dioxygen is also present in water, it comes from the air and is breathed by fish.

Pure water is the one that contains only one substance. To obtain pure water, distillation is used. This water is used in laboratories or irons, but not for drinking every day. Pure water has a neutral pH between zero and seven.

The water we drink is therefore a mixture of water, salts and gas. Distilled water is the purest, but it contains almost nothing dissolved.

Key vocabulary

  • copper sulphate anhydrous : A white powder used to detect water in a liquid.
  • evaporation : The process by which water passes from liquid to vapour.
  • pH : An indicator of acidity or the basis of a solution.
  • Carbon dioxide : A colourless and odourless gas, also called carbon dioxide.
  • mixture : A compound consisting of several substances that have not mixed chemically.
