Level 1 · simplified reading

Alexandria was a city in the ancient world. At night, a boat sees a light on the coast. This light comes from a very high tower. It is the lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the seven wonders. The lighthouse was built under King Ptolemy First. The work lasted 15 years. The cost was eight hundred talents, or about seventeen tons of money. The tower was one hundred and three metres long, like a thirty-storey building. It stood on the island of Pharos, in front of the great port. For a thousand and six hundred years she guided the ships. On August 8, three hundred and three, an earthquake brought down the lighthouse. The ruins were used as a quarry and then sank under the sea. Today researchers are looking for answers. They study texts, currencies, vestiges and a digital model. Their goal is to know what the lighthouse looked like, how it was built and why it fell.

Level 2 · standard reading

Alexandria, in Egypt, more than two thousand years ago, sees a boat approaching a dangerous coast, suddenly illuminated by a huge tower. This tower is the lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world, now gone, and the subject of our investigation.

The site began about two hundred and ninety-seven B.C. under King Ptolemy First, but the lighthouse was only completed under his successor Ptolemy Second, between two hundred and eighty-three and two hundred and eighty-one B.C. It takes only 15 years to build this tower, which is about one hundred and three metres high, the height of a thirty-storey building. The cost is estimated at eight hundred talents, each talent weighing just over twenty-one kilos, which is about seventeen tons of silver, much more than the Parthenon which had cost a little more than twelve tons.

The lighthouse, located on the island of Pharos in front of the port of Alexandria, dominates the Grand Port and inspires other cities, such as Ostia where Emperor Claude has built a tower "like the lighthouse of Alexandria". For a thousand and six hundred years he remained standing, then on August eight, one thousand three hundred three earthquake brought him down, destroying one third of the city. The ruins are reused as a quarry before sinking under the waters.

Today, Céalex researchers use four families of clues – texts, coins, vestiges and digital models – to reconstruct the lighthouse. They ask three questions: what he looked like, how he was built and why he fell. Their mission invites students to check the calculations, classify the indices and formulate their first hypothesis on the shape of the lighthouse.

Level 3 · in-depth reading

On the night of Alexandria, more than two thousand years ago, a ship approached a coast dotted with reefs. In the distance, a light illuminates a gigantic tower: the lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world. This construction was designed to guide ships along a dangerous coast, report the city from a long distance and control the entrance to the Grand Port.

The site begins about two hundred and ninety-seven before Jesus Christ, during the reign of Ptolemy First, and ends under Ptolemy Second, between two hundred and eighty-three and two hundred and eighty-one before Jesus Christ. In just 15 years, the builders raise a tower of about one hundred and three meters, comparable to a building of about thirty floors. The cost, according to Pliny the Elder, amounts to eight hundred talents, or about seventeen tons of money, which exceeds the budget of the Parthenon of Athens.

Its unique shape, composed of three superimposed sections crowned by a statue, inspires other cities: in Ostia, Emperor Claude had a tower erected "like the lighthouse of Alexandria". This singularity responds to the constraints of day and night visibility, wind and sea resistance, and longevity.

After a thousand and six hundred years of presence, on August 8, a thousand and three hundred three, a great earthquake struck the city, dropped the lighthouse and destroyed a third of Alexandria. Abandoned, the building becomes a stone quarry and then dark under the waters of the Mediterranean.

Today, the researchers at Céalex gather four families of clues: ancient texts, the engraved coins of the lighthouse, the remains under water and a three-dimensional digital model. These elements help to reconstruct the appearance of the lighthouse, understand its construction methods and explain the reasons for its fall, in order to better understand the ingenuity of the builders of ancient times.
